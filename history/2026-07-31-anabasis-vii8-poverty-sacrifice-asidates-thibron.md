## Documentary movement

The army sails to Lampsacus, where Eucleides questions Xenophon about the wealth acquired during the expedition. Xenophon says he barely has enough to reach home unless he sells his horse and personal property. During sacrifice to Apollo, Eucleides accepts the poverty claim, says Xenophon is himself an obstacle to wealth, and identifies neglected sacrifice to Zeus Meilichios. Xenophon performs his family’s customary holocaust of swine at Ophrynium; favorable signs are followed that day by gifts for the army and the gratuitous restoration of his fifty-daric horse.

The army marches through the Troad and Mysia to Pergamus. Hellas supplies intelligence and guides for a night expedition against Asidates. Basias reads the victims as highly favorable, and Xenophon selects trusted officers whom he wishes to benefit. Roughly six hundred additional volunteers are excluded from the expected spoil.

The first attack fails against a fortified tower. Relief forces answer beacon signals. The Hellenes retreat in a hollow square with animals and captives, receive aid from Gongylus and Procles, and cross the Carcasus under missiles. Nearly half are wounded, including Agasias, but they return safely with about two hundred captives and sheep for sacrifice.

After another sacrifice Xenophon leads the whole army by night. Asidates leaves the tower for villages below Parthenium and is captured with his household, horses, and property. The narrator calls this the literal fulfillment of the earlier victims. At Pergamus the Laconians, officers, generals, and soldiers unite to grant Xenophon first choice of property. Thibron then receives and incorporates the army for war against Tissaphernes and Pharnabazus.

## Safeguards

- Poverty is narrated evidence against accumulated wealth, not proof of every financial detail.
- Favorable sacrifice and subsequent events remain sequentially connected without certifying divine causation.
- The horse is restored through voluntary generosity, not assigned from common army property.
- Hellas’s intelligence and Basias’s interpretation are attributed reports.
- The first expedition’s prediction of easy prey is preserved beside its failed assault and heavy wounds.
- The stated disciplinary reason for retaining property during retreat does not erase the booty interest.
- Asidates’s capture is not certified as just or lawful.
- The collective reward to Xenophon is not a complete audited distribution.
- The narrative ends with renewed war under Spartan command rather than narrated homecoming.
- Secondary comparison remains deferred.

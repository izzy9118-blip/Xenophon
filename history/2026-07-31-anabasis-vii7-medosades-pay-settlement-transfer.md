## Documentary movement preserved

The army quarters in villages granted by Seuthes to Medosades. Medosades arrives with a powerful Odrysian and about thirty horsemen, orders the Greeks out, and threatens retaliation. Xenophon answers before reliable witnesses by recalling Greek conquest, benefaction, and the transfer of the army to the Spartan commanders. The Odrysian repudiates the expulsion demand in shame and withdraws with most of the mounted party.

Xenophon uses Medosades's summons to bring Charminus and Polynicus into the wage dispute. The Spartans make payment a condition of departure and threaten oathbreakers. Medosades rejects Xenophon's proposal for a local ballot and instead seeks an embassy to Seuthes while begging that the villages not be burned.

Before Seuthes, Xenophon argues that payment protects the ruler's reputation, the credibility of his word, the stability of coerced subjects, the peace of his newly acquired territory, and his future ability to recruit allies. He calls Seuthes and the gods to witness that he accepted nothing privately and would not take his own dues unless the soldiers received theirs. He names valour, justice, and generosity as the threefold grace most fitting for a prince.

Seuthes promises repayment, renews the personal offer of fortified places and one-thousand-man service, and then supplies one talent, six hundred cattle, four thousand sheep, one hundred twenty slaves, hostages, and an escort. Xenophon treats the property as a fraction, turns it over to Charminus and Polynicus for official distribution, and holds aloof when the appointed vendors incur blame. Although preparing to return home, he is asked by camp authorities to remain until he conducts the army to Thibron.

## Safeguards

- Property granted to Medosades is not treated as erasing prior wage obligations.
- Greek conquest and benefaction claims are preserved as Xenophon's speech, not certified justice.
- The Odrysian's shame is testimony, not independent adjudication.
- Truthful speech as political power is preserved as Xenophon's argument.
- Forced subjection and desire for liberty are not converted into endorsement of either ruler or rebels.
- Xenophon's oath and refusal of private settlement are evidence within the narrative, not final proof of motive.
- The mixed settlement is not declared equal to the full debt without valuation.
- Vendor blame is preserved without assigning unreported guilt.
- Translator wording is not treated as unmediated Greek evidence.
- Secondary comparison remains deferred.

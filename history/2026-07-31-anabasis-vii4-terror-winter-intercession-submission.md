## Documentary movement

1. Seuthes burns every house in the captured villages to inspire terror and sends the booty to Heracleides at Perinthus for sale toward future pay.
2. The Greeks suffer extreme cold, frozen water and wine, and frostbitten noses and ears, thereby learning the practical logic of Thracian winter clothing.
3. Prisoners carry a threat of burned villages, destroyed corn, and starvation unless the Thynians return and obey. Women, children, and older men descend; younger men remain near the hills.
4. Seuthes and Xenophon lead the youngest heavy infantry in a night raid. Most inhabitants escape, but Seuthes shoots those he captures.
5. Episthenes appeals through Xenophon for a handsome captive youth. He offers his own life, the youth asks that neither die, and Seuthes laughs and spares both.
6. Seuthes disperses the army among villages to deny subsistence to the mountaineers. Xenophon warns that the quarters are insecure.
7. Mountaineers seek truce and hostages while using the parley to inspect the quarters.
8. At night householders guide an assault with spears, clubs, and fire. The attackers call Xenophon by name and threaten to roast him alive.
9. Silanus sounds the trumpet. The armed Greeks break out, kill or capture attackers, and suffer two wounded captains but no deaths.
10. Seuthes arrives with seven troopers and a trumpeter. The next day he transfers elderly hostages, returns with a tripled force, and receives Thynian submission.
11. Xenophon regards enslavement as sufficient punishment and recommends holding capable troublemakers rather than old men as hostages.

## Preserved distinctions

- Terror as an avowed policy is distinct from incidental destruction in battle.
- Sending booty for sale toward future pay is not actual payment or transparent accounting.
- Thracian environmental knowledge is inferred from clothing after Greek injury; it is not a general cultural judgment.
- Coerced descent of noncombatants is distinct from complete submission by the younger men.
- Xenophon's intercession is distinct from sovereign power to spare; Seuthes retains the final decision.
- Episthenes's courage and eros are joined in the episode without establishing a universal doctrine of beauty or virtue.
- Existing hostages do not guarantee security, and a request for truce may conceal reconnaissance.
- The Greeks rescue themselves before Seuthes arrives; his later assistance remains real but limited.
- Old hostages, capable hostages, and political adhesion perform different functions.
- Loss of freedom is recorded as punishment without certifying its justice.
- Translated wording is not treated as unmediated Greek evidence, and secondary comparison remains deferred.

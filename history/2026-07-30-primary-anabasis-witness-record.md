# Primary Anabasis Witness Admission Record

Date: 2026-07-30

The owner supplied a searchable PDF of Project Gutenberg ebook 1170, Xenophon's *Anabasis* in the English translation of H. G. Dakyns, after the repository requested a primary Xenophontic witness.

The witness is admitted as the repository's first primary translation witness. It authorizes independent sequential reconstruction of the *Anabasis*, beginning with Book I, chapter 1. It does not authenticate the English translation as the Greek text and does not remove the need for an original-language witness or critical edition.

The PDF is a 168-page Safari/iOS capture of Project Gutenberg HTML. The repository records its metadata, one-based PDF locator map, owner-supplied filename, byte size, and SHA-256 digest. The binary file is not deposited in the repository.

`XEN-PRI-RU-001` through `XEN-PRI-RU-010` reconstruct *Anabasis* Book I in full as a sequential draft pending owner review. `XEN-PRI-RU-011` through `XEN-PRI-RU-016` reconstruct Book II in full, also pending owner review. These are draft-coverage milestones only; neither book has received owner adoption or final textual certification.

The Book II sequence preserves the victory dispute, the demand for disarmament, the Theopompus/Xenophon attribution variant, Clearchus's unelected practical leadership, the Greek-Asian oath, the possible omission of the sacrificial wolf, the narrator's judgment that fortune proved a better general, the truce-and-market compact, Ariaeus's political detachment, false bridge warnings, the conjectural second Xenophon appearance, the tiara-upon-the-heart image, the ignored warning against excessive trust, the seizure and killing of the Greek command, and the opposed accusations of perjury.

`XEN-PRI-RU-016` closes Book II through comparative portraits of Clearchus, Proxenus, Menon, Agias, and Socrates. It preserves first-person narratorial judgment, collective testimony, recorded saying, possible fabrication, reported death, the contrast among command through fear, honorable friendship, and complicity in wrongdoing, and the tension between the opening collective statement of decapitation and the later distinct account of Menon's punishment.

`XEN-PRI-RU-017` begins Book III with the army's despair, Xenophon's retrospective account of joining the expedition, consultation with Socrates and Delphi, the omitted oracle gods, the Pisidian pretext, the dream of Zeus's lightning and the burning paternal house, waking self-interrogation, two leadership speeches, Apollonides's interrupted dissent and exclusion, the midnight officer council, and replacement of the five lost generals. Xenophon is introduced as a non-officer, appears in the witness through a first-person reference to "my friendship with Cyrus," acts before formal office, and is then elected to replace Proxenus. The unit preserves the Book III synopsis and Grote note as witness paratext, not primary Xenophontic narration.

`XEN-PRI-RU-018` continues Book III through the dawn assembly. Cheirisophus and Cleanor frame the choice as resistance rather than capture and denounce Persian and Asiatic betrayal. Xenophon appears in his finest war attire, interprets an unnamed sneeze as an omen from Zeus the Saviour, secures collective vows and a battle hymn, invokes ancestral victories and Greek freedom, and reverses fears concerning deserters, cavalry, guides, markets, rivers, and isolation. The unit preserves the conditional settlement argument, Lotus-eater warning, poor-Greek prosperity claim, proposed destruction of wagons and tents, intensified discipline, invitation to private soldiers, two votes by raised hands, and the experimental hollow-square formation. Notes concerning Skobelef, Homer, Herodotus, Grote, battle dating, the Lotus-eaters, and colonisation remain witness paratext.

`XEN-PRI-RU-019` executes the assembly's decisions and records the first tactical correction under the reconstituted command. The Greeks burn wagons and tents, redistribute necessary goods, and march with baggage and noncombatants enclosed. Mithridates approaches as a professed friend, argues for dependence on Artaxerxes, and is identified by the narrator as a disguised agent under surveillance from Tissaphernes. The generals answer with a policy of minimal damage if allowed passage, then resolve on open war without truce or herald after recognizing corruption attempts and Nicarchus's desertion. Mithridates returns with mounted and missile troops, approaches as friendly, and attacks the Greek rear beyond effective Greek range. Xenophon leads an unsuccessful pursuit, is criticized by Cheirisophus and the eldest generals, admits the error, and converts the failure into a proposal for Rhodian slingers and cavalry. Two hundred slingers are enrolled, fifty cavalry qualify, and Lycius son of Polystratus is appointed commander. The unit preserves the unresolved identity of Nicarchus and the editorial question concerning the lead-bullet parenthesis.

`XEN-PRI-RU-020` covers *Anabasis* III.4. The registered witness span is one-based PDF pages 61-66 because page 61 contains the closing lines of III.3 and the visible heading for III.4 begins on page 62; the chapter ends on page 66 when Xenophon's detachment reaches the summit first. A prepared infantry-and-cavalry pursuit defeats Mithridates's enlarged attack, captures eighteen cavalry, and is followed by Greek mutilation of enemy bodies intended to terrify. The retreat then passes the ruined Median cities Larissa and Mespila, whose conquest traditions and modern identifications remain separate from the primary narrative. Rhodian slingers and bowmen drive Tissaphernes's great mixed force out of range, but high terrain later renders the Greek light troops ineffective until the army secures parallel high ground. The generals revise the hollow square by creating six flexible lochoi, appoint eight surgeons for the wounded, exploit Persian evening withdrawal, and answer a Persian forced night march with a race for the commanding summit. Cheirisophus and Xenophon divide responsibility between rear security and the assault. Xenophon leads because he is younger; after Soteridas protests the inequality of mounted command, Xenophon carries his shield while the soldiers coerce Soteridas to resume it. The unit preserves the mutilation, lochoi, picked-corps, and Soteridas manuscript questions without resolving them.

`XEN-PRI-RU-021` closes Book III. The Greeks hold the summit but dispersed foragers are killed, cattle are captured, and Tissaphernes attempts to burn the provisioned villages. Xenophon treats Persian burning as an admission that the country is now Greek and proposes protecting "our country"; Cheirisophus instead proposes reciprocal burning. Deep despondency follows when the command confronts high mountains and a Tigris too deep for their spears. An unnamed Rhodian offers, for one talent, to carry four thousand hoplites at a time over a bridge of two thousand inflated skins, but the generals reject the proposal because enemy cavalry controls the far bank. The Greeks reverse toward Babylon, burn villages behind them, cross-examine prisoners on four directional routes, and compartmentalize knowledgeable prisoners without revealing their intention. Prisoner testimony depicts the Carduchians as warlike, outside royal control, and dangerous enough to have destroyed a reported royal army of one hundred twenty thousand, while also capable of temporary truce with the satrap. The generals choose the Carduchian hills toward Armenia, sacrifice, fear advance occupation of the pass, and order the army to prepare after supper for immediate movement. The Karduchian note and Book IV retrospective synopsis remain witness paratext.

`XEN-PRI-RU-022` begins Book IV with the night entry into Carduchian territory. Cheirisophus leads the van with all light troops while Xenophon follows with the heavy rearguard without light troops because rear attack is not expected. The first summit is reached unseen, but Carduchian families abandon their homes and the Greeks' selective restraint-taking necessary provisions while leaving bronze vessels and refraining from pursuit-does not produce friendly passage. A small force attacks the hindmost men at dusk, and the narrator judges that a larger force might have annihilated a large part of the army. At dawn the command reduces weak baggage animals and releases recently captured slaves for logistical reasons, proclaims the decision, and confiscates retained captives and property, while some soldiers secretly keep a desired boy or woman. Under storm and food pressure, conflicting pace orders through narrow terrain make the rear resemble a rout; Cleonymus and Basias are killed and cannot be recovered for burial. Xenophon rebukes Cheirisophus, who defends his haste as an effort to seize the only reported exit. Xenophon produces two prisoners captured in an ambush. The first maintains denial under unspecified terrors and is killed before the second; the second then reveals a beast-passable road and a col that must be occupied first. Aristonymus, Agasias, Callimachus, and Aristeas volunteer for the special expedition. The Book IV synopsis remains paratext, and the coercion, execution, captive status, and route intelligence remain explicitly unresolved.

`XEN-PRI-RU-023` covers the volunteer storming operation of IV.2. A bound guide leads two thousand men around the visible pass while Xenophon mounts a diversion. The flanking force mistakes a lower outpost for the summit, then attacks at dawn under mist while Cheirisophus and other divisions climb by the marked and pathless routes. Xenophon's successive ridge seizures open the only baggage road but expose the first ridge; Carduchians retake it, kill Cephisodorus and Amphicrates with others, and drive Archagoras into flight. Xenophon negotiates for the dead on condition that Greek houses are not burned, but the agreement's scope remains uncertain when gathered Carduchians attack during the descent. Xenophon's shield-bearer leaves with his shield, while Eurylochus shares his own shield and escorts him to safety. The Greeks recover the dead, return the guide, perform available customary honors, and continue without a guide through reciprocal relief of van and rear. Carduchian long-bow technology, penetrating arrows, Greek reuse as javelins, and Cretan utility remain separately documented.

`XEN-PRI-RU-024` covers IV.3 and corrects the chapter's page range to one-based PDF pages 75-79 after exact visual inspection showed the final sentences on page 79. The army rests above the Centrites after seven days of Carduchian fighting, then finds Armenian, Mardian, and Chaldaean forces blocking the far bank while Carduchians remain behind. A visible crossing proves too deep and slippery. Xenophon reports a dream in which self-falling fetters free his legs; the generals obtain favorable sacrifice, and two unnamed young men then report a shallow ford discovered while gathering brushwood. Xenophon and Cheirisophus offer libations, and the command divides the army so Cheirisophus leads half across while Xenophon protects the second half and the baggage passage. Cheirisophus conducts a ceremonial main crossing under sacrifice, paean, shouting, and women's chant. Xenophon feigns a return toward the visible crossing, helping dislodge enemy cavalry, then forms the exposed rear against descending Carduchians. Through missile support, close-range charge, paean, bugle, preserved ranks, and rapid reversal, the rearguard crosses with a few wounds. A supporting detachment carried beyond its orders by valor suffers additional wounds. The Centrites identification, diabainein explanation, wreath-or-cloak question, comrade-women gloss, and pursuit-shout alternatives remain paratext or unresolved textual matters.

`XEN-PRI-RU-025` covers IV.4, one-based PDF pages 79-81. After crossing the Centrites, the Greeks move through an Armenian plain to satrapal settlements with abundant provisions, pass the reported sources of the Tigris and the Teleboas, and enter Western Armenia under Tiribazus. Through an interpreter he proposes a treaty: he will abstain from injury if the Greeks do not burn his houses and take only needed provisions. The generals accept, but Tiribazus continues following at armed distance. Snow and uncertain intelligence produce repeated reversals between dispersed village billets and concentrated open quarters. A heavy fall covers weapons, men, and baggage animals; Xenophon rises without his cloak and begins splitting wood, and his example induces the soldiers to rise, make fires, and oil themselves. When the army returns under cover, men who had previously burned houses receive poorer quarters. Democrates, praised for distinguishing fact from fiction, reports no watchfires but captures a Persian carrying a bow, quiver, and sagaris. The prisoner reports Tiribazus, Chalybian and Taochian mercenaries, and a planned attack at the sole high-pass defile. The generals immediately advance with the prisoner as guide and leave Sophaenetus guarding the remnant. Light infantry rushes the enemy entrenchment without waiting for the heavy infantry, kills some fugitives, and captures twenty horses, Tiribazus's tent, silver-footed couches, goblets, and persons calling themselves butlers and bakers. The heavy-infantry generals recall the force to protect the camp remnant. The treaty form, prisoner conditions, force size, Democrates ethnicon variants, clothing note, casualty totals, captive status, and ownership of spoils remain unresolved.

`XEN-PRI-RU-026` covers IV.5, PDF pages 81-84. The army crosses the threatened pass before enemy occupation, reaches and crosses the Euphrates, and then suffers deep snow, north wind, loss of animals, enslaved persons, and about thirty soldiers. An unnamed seer recommends sacrifice to Boreas, after which the narrator reports a diminished blast. Scarce fire produces exchange of warmth for food. Xenophon learns that collapsed men suffer boulimia, requisitions food from the baggage train, and distributes it personally or through agents. Cheirisophus reaches a fortified Armenian village through a Persian cover story, while weaker men remain outside without food or fire and some die. Pursuers seize disabled animals; snow blindness, frostbite, failed footwear, and exhaustion leave soldiers behind. Xenophon pleads, grows angry, and then uses a noisy rearguard feint to protect invalids who refuse to move. After reunion, villages are assigned by lot; Polycrates captures villagers, the headman, a newly married daughter, and seventeen royal tribute horses. Underground houses contain animals, grain, vegetables, and strong barley wine drunk through reeds. Xenophon hosts the headman under guard, promises protection and compensation, obtains route knowledge, gives him an old horse for sacrifice after learning he is a priest of the sun, distributes colts to officers, and receives advice to wrap animal feet for snow travel. The chapter preserves textual variants, medical uncertainty, coercion, capture status, sacrifice, and the inseparability of hospitality and custody.

`XEN-PRI-RU-027` covers IV.6, PDF pages 84-87. Xenophon transfers the unfettered headman-guide to Cheirisophus while the headman's household is left safe except for a young son retained under Episthenes as conditional security for good guidance. Cheirisophus strikes the guide when no villages are reached but fails to bind him, enabling his nighttime escape and leaving the son behind. The narrator calls this combination of ill-treatment and neglect the sole ground of difference between Cheirisophus and Xenophon during the march. Episthenes is then reported to conceive a passion for the youth, take him home, and find him a faithful friend; the Greek terminology, consent, and status remain unresolved. At a pass held by Chalybes, Taochians, and Phasianians, Cheirisophus deploys from column into line and convenes the generals. Cleanor favors immediate frontal attack; Xenophon instead seeks the fewest wounds and least loss of good men through night seizure of unoccupied high ground and a frontal feint. Xenophon and Cheirisophus exchange jokes about Spartan education in theft and Athenian theft of public money; these remain attributed civic stereotypes, not certified institutional facts. Captured rear-followers provide route intelligence. Cheirisophus preserves Xenophon's rear command, while Aristonymus and Nicomachus lead volunteer heavy and light troops, with watchfires as signal. The main body advances as diversion, the detachment takes the heights at night, and after dawn sacrifice the two forces advance in parallel. Defeat of the enemy high-ground section causes the road force to flee. The Greeks destroy captured wicker shields, sacrifice, set up a trophy, and descend to provisioned villages. The Phasis identification and literal missile note remain paratext.

`XEN-PRI-RU-028` covers IV.7 and corrects the chapter boundary to PDF pages 87-90 after visual inspection showed that the cairn, guide payment, route instruction, and dismissal occur on page 90 before heading VIII. Provision failure in Taochian territory leads Cheirisophus to attack a river-encircled stronghold. After repeated stone-inflicted injuries, Xenophon proposes using tree cover and deliberate exposure to exhaust the defenders' ammunition. Callimachus draws out wagon-loads of stones, but competitive honor disrupts the staged plan when Agasias rushes without summons and Aristonymus and Eurylochus race through the fire. The fortress falls, followed by the reported mass deaths of women, infants, and men; Aeneas is dragged over the cliff while attempting a rescue, few human prisoners are taken, and abundant livestock is seized. The Chalybes are described as the bravest close-quarter opponents encountered, with distinctive armor, weapons, head display, singing, and dancing, but the report remains unauthenticated ethnography. After Scythenian resupply and arrival at Gymnias, a guide stakes his life on showing the sea within five days and urges the Greeks to burn and harry a district hostile to his own community. At Theches the front ranks see the sea. Xenophon initially interprets the growing cry as another attack because the rear is fighting inhabitants amid a burning landscape, then rides forward and hears the acclamation. The army runs to the summit, embraces and weeps, raises a cairn under an anonymous order, dedicates captured materials, and rewards the guide from the common store and with private rings before his nocturnal departure. The Chalybian, Gymnias, Theches, spear, and sacred-mountain notes remain paratext.

Books I, II, and III have complete sequential draft coverage pending owner review. Book IV draft coverage now extends through IV.7.

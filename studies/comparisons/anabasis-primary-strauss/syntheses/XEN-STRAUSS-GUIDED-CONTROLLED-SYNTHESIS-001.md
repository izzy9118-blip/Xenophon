# Xenophon Revealing Through the *Anabasis*

## Strauss-guided controlled synthesis

## Governing direction

Xenophon is the revealing author. The younger Cyrus, the older Cyrus, Socrates, and the Xenophon who acts and speaks in the *Anabasis* are differentiated presentations through which Xenophon reveals his inquiry. They do not stand outside his work and supply him with standards. Strauss guides the recovery of the concealed order among Xenophon's presentations without replacing their documentary evidence with a doctrine.

This synthesis therefore distinguishes four levels throughout:

1. what the admitted English *Anabasis* witness shows;
2. what Strauss explicitly argues;
3. what can be inferred by controlled comparison;
4. what must remain unresolved.

## I. The younger Cyrus and the misleading beginning

The work begins from the younger Cyrus's dynastic ambition. His expedition is assembled through money, friendship, private arrangements, unequal knowledge, and misleading local causes. The Greek force succeeds tactically at Cunaxa, but Cyrus dies. The success of the soldiers is separated from the political end that had gathered them.

Strauss begins with the authentic title and the ascent of the younger Cyrus, yet the failed ascent redirects the reader toward Xenophon's own ascent. Xenophon's presentation of the younger Cyrus reveals the attraction and instability of political action founded on charismatic command, personal allegiance, wealth, secrecy, and ambition. The younger Cyrus is not merely a failed predecessor. His attraction establishes the political field into which Xenophon enters.

The synthesis does not decide whether the later comparison with the older Cyrus corrects this attraction, elevates it, or reveals what was latent in it.

## II. Proxenos, fatherland, and Socratic counsel

Xenophon does not enter the expedition by announcing an independent political project. Proxenos invites him. The friend who seeks honor, power, and wealth presents the younger Cyrus as possibly better than fatherland. Through Proxenos, Xenophon reveals the political force of friendship and the possibility that foreign service may outrank attachment to one's own city.

Socrates' counsel and Xenophon's consultation at Delphi do not arrest the movement toward action. The question to the god assumes the journey rather than asking whether it should be undertaken. Xenophon reveals a man who still acknowledges Socratic judgment but has already begun to direct himself.

The relation among friendship, ambition, fatherland, and philosophic counsel remains open.

## III. Economic art and the material basis of rule

After Cyrus's death, political life becomes inseparable from material administration. The army must secure food, markets, pay, transport, weapons, animals, booty, medical care, routes, and discipline. Assemblies, elected offices, specialized troops, punishments, rewards, and negotiated agreements are not secondary details. They are conditions of collective survival.

Strauss distinguishes Xenophon from Socrates through self-direction and the economic art. The primary reconstruction independently confirms that Xenophon's rise occurs through material governance as well as speech and courage. Xenophon reveals that political knowledge must organize bodies, goods, offices, time, and necessity.

Material competence is necessary for rule in the narrative, but it is never sufficient to establish legitimacy, justice, wisdom, or piety.

## IV. Xenophon's narrated ascent

The narrated Xenophon becomes speaker, initiator, elected general, tactician, disciplinarian, negotiator, defendant, and contested leader. His authority is real but never absolute. It exists among other generals, captains, assemblies, cities, soldiers, allies, and Spartan officers.

Through this narrated self, Xenophon reveals political knowledge as the ability to join persuasion and force, correction and obedience, provision and punishment, ambition and restraint. Yet the author does not allow the successful actor simply to certify himself. Xenophon is accused, tried, contradicted, resisted, and repeatedly compelled to explain his conduct.

The revealing author, the narrator, the remembered speaker, and the historical actor must not be collapsed into one transparent figure.

## V. Piety, wiliness, and political necessity

Dreams, sacrifices, omens, oaths, deception, concealment, endurance, rescue, and harsh necessity recur throughout the work. Strauss joins piety and wiliness rather than treating them as mutually exclusive.

Xenophon reveals that effective political action may require tactical deception, secrecy, punishment, and harm while also presenting sacrifice and oath as politically serious. The relation is not resolved into hypocrisy, harmony, or a simple hierarchy. The composition makes their coexistence a problem.

The synthesis therefore leaves open whether piety governs political action, instruments it, qualifies it, or remains inseparably joined to it.

## VI. The older Cyrus, justice, and the hidden comparison

Strauss explicitly compares Xenophon with the older Cyrus and Socrates. Justice is called ambiguous. Manly justice benefits friends and harms enemies; Socratic justice refuses to harm anyone even in a little thing. Xenophon is placed between the older Cyrus and Socrates, yet Strauss also says that Xenophon is not near the older Cyrus.

The direction of revelation is decisive. The older Cyrus does not supply Xenophon with a standard from outside Xenophon's work. Through his presentation of the older Cyrus, Xenophon reveals one form of political wisdom: knowledge of rule joined to inheritance, prescription, established authority, manly justice, and the capacity for cruelty.

Read beside the *Anabasis*, this presentation illuminates Xenophon's harder political problem. The narrated Xenophon demonstrates knowledge and ability without hereditary title. He uses punishment and force without approaching the older Cyrus's narrated cruelty. He must repeatedly transform competence into accepted authority through persuasion, election, public defense, success, and restraint.

The comparison does not settle whether the older Cyrus corrects, fulfills, elevates, or exposes what Xenophon first revealed through the younger Cyrus. Justice governs the ambiguity between the two Cyruses.

## VII. Founding, monarchy, and Byzantium

The possibilities of founding and monarchy appear repeatedly, but they become concrete together at Byzantium. Xenophon confronts a city, triremes, money, soldiers, unpaid claims, Spartan power, and an army capable of violence against a wholly innocent Greek city.

Xenophon does not seize the city or establish unauthorized rule. His restraint is revealed in deed and therefore bears more evidentiary weight than a verbal denial of ambition. Yet the deed cannot certify pure motive or complete justice. Prudence, fear of Sparta, concern for Greece, fatherland, kin, self-preservation, legitimacy, and justice remain mixed.

Byzantium is therefore not proof that Xenophon lacked political ambition. It is the strongest test of how ambition, political capacity, and restraint are joined in his self-presentation.

## VIII. Thibron and the final coda

The primary narrative ends institutionally when the army is incorporated under Thibron into renewed Persian war. Its autonomous political existence ends even though private debts, reputations, purposes, and Xenophon's personal homecoming remain unresolved.

Strauss's final textual movement must be preserved in exact order:

1. the question whether Socrates' apprehension was vindicated;
2. renewed Persian war for booty;
3. Book VII's exceptional density of gods and formal oaths.

The ending does not offer a simple return from politics to philosophy, from acquisition to piety, or from independence to legitimate authority. War, booty, gods, oath, and institutional incorporation remain together under the Socrates–Cyrus examination.

Thibron is the primary institutional endpoint. Strauss's coda is the final textual emphasis. They belong to one examination and are not two equal closures.

## Controlled conclusion

The *Anabasis* cannot be reduced to praise of command, confession of ambition, a return narrative, or disguised philosophical autobiography. Through the younger Cyrus, the older Cyrus, Socrates, and the narrated Xenophon, Xenophon composes an indirect examination of political attraction, legitimacy, knowledge, cruelty, justice, fatherland, material governance, piety, and restraint.

Strauss's achievement is to uncover the movement of this examination without eliminating its concealment. Xenophon's self-presentation may suggest the closest approximation known to him of the coexistence of manly and Socratic virtue, but the synthesis does not identify this as achieved fact or final teaching. It preserves the difference between achievement, aspiration, presentation, and unresolved claim.

## Governing correction

The *Anabasis* is the center of this synthesis. Xenophon reveals through its title, sequence, speeches, deeds, silences, and narrated self-presentation. Strauss illuminates that inquiry by bringing it into relation with Xenophon’s presentation of the older Cyrus in the *Education of Cyrus* and with Xenophon’s Socratic presentations.

The older Cyrus is therefore neither a figure contained in the *Anabasis* nor a standard supplied to Xenophon from outside. Xenophon presents the older Cyrus elsewhere in his corpus. Strauss uses that Xenophontic presentation to ask questions of the Xenophon presented in the *Anabasis*.

Those questions must remain questions. Strauss asks whether knowledge of rule needs an “iron alloy” in order to become legitimate or politically viable, whether prescription is indispensable to non-tyrannical government, and what the ambiguity of justice discloses. He does not give inheritance or prescription as a finished doctrine of legitimate rule.

The decisive direction is Xenophon’s. By presenting his narrated self between the older Cyrus and Socrates, Xenophon presents to the reader not indecision but the problem of justice.

## I. The younger Cyrus and the misleading beginning

The expedition begins in concealed dynastic ambition, money, friendship, unequal knowledge, and distributed pretexts. The Greeks succeed tactically at Cunaxa, but the younger Cyrus dies. Their excellence is separated from the political purpose that gathered them.

Strauss begins from the authentic title, “Cyrus’ Ascent,” but Cyrus’s failed ascent redirects attention toward Xenophon’s ascent. Within the *Anabasis*, Xenophon’s presentation of the younger Cyrus reveals political attraction organized by friendship, wealth, secrecy, ambition, generosity, and a claim to rule dependent on one man’s survival.

The later older-Cyrus comparison does not erase this beginning. It leaves open whether the younger Cyrus’s attraction is corrected, elevated, or retrospectively exposed.

## II. Proxenos, fatherland, and Socratic counsel

Xenophon enters through Proxenos rather than through an independently announced political project. Proxenos seeks honor, power, and wealth and presents the younger Cyrus as possibly better than fatherland. Friendship becomes the route by which political attraction reaches Xenophon.

Socrates’ counsel and the Delphic consultation do not arrest the movement. Xenophon asks how to undertake the journey rather than whether he should undertake it. Xenophon presents a man who acknowledges Socratic judgment while already directing himself toward action.

The relation among friendship, ambition, fatherland, and philosophic counsel remains open.

## III. Economic art and the material basis of rule

After Cyrus’s death, political survival depends on food, markets, pay, transport, booty, offices, assemblies, discipline, punishment, reward, and tactical adaptation. These are not incidental details. They are the material conditions under which the army can continue to exist as a collective body.

Strauss distinguishes Xenophon from Socrates through self-direction and the economic art. The independent primary reconstruction confirms that Xenophon’s ascent occurs through material governance as much as through speech and courage.

Xenophon reveals that command requires knowledge of bodies, goods, routes, time, offices, and necessity. Yet material competence never proves justice, legitimacy, wisdom, or piety.

## IV. Xenophon’s narrated ascent

The narrated Xenophon becomes initiator, speaker, elected general, tactician, disciplinarian, negotiator, defendant, and contested leader. His authority is real but distributed among generals, captains, assemblies, cities, soldiers, allies, and Spartan officers.

Through this narrated self, Xenophon reveals political knowledge as the ability to join persuasion and force, correction and obedience, provision and punishment, ambition and restraint. The revealing author nevertheless refuses to let the successful actor certify himself. Xenophon is accused, resisted, tried, contradicted, and repeatedly required to explain his conduct.

The author, narrator, remembered speaker, and historical actor must remain distinct.

## V. Piety, wiliness, and political necessity

Dreams, sacrifices, omens, oaths, concealment, deception, endurance, rescue, and harsh necessity recur throughout the work. Strauss joins piety and wiliness rather than treating them as simple opposites.

Xenophon reveals that effective political action may require deception, secrecy, punishment, and harm while presenting sacrifice and oath as politically serious. Their coexistence is not resolved into hypocrisy or harmony. It remains part of the inquiry.

The sources do not settle whether piety governs political action, instruments it, qualifies it, or remains inseparably entangled with it.

## VI. Cross-work comparison and the problem of justice

At this point Strauss moves beyond the *Anabasis* without abandoning it. He brings Xenophon’s self-presentation into relation with the hero Xenophon presents in the *Education of Cyrus*, the older Cyrus.

The works must not be collapsed. The *Anabasis* presents Xenophon commanding without hereditary kingship, using punishment without the older Cyrus’s narrated extremity, and repeatedly securing consent, election, public defense, recognized authority, and restraint. The *Education of Cyrus* presents the older Cyrus with hereditary advantages Xenophon lacked.

Strauss then asks. Granted that knowledge of how to rule gives the highest right to rule, does such knowledge require an “iron alloy” to become legitimate or politically viable? Is prescription indispensable to non-tyrannical government? These questions expose the distance between knowledge and accepted political right; they do not establish inheritance or prescription as a settled model.

Strauss next calls justice ambiguous. Manly justice benefits friends and harms enemies. Socratic justice refuses to harm anyone even in a little thing. Xenophon possesses the justice of a man but not the justice of Socrates. Yet his place is not near the older Cyrus, whose cruelty is of another degree.

Xenophon stands somewhere between the older Cyrus and Socrates. By this position, Strauss says, Xenophon presents to us not a lack of decisiveness but the problem of justice. The virtue of the man permits command, punishment, harm, and the possible emancipation of cruelty. Socratic virtue points beyond harm. Each points toward or requires the other, yet they cannot coexist in plenitude in one human being.

The older Cyrus therefore supplies no answer to Xenophon. Xenophon supplies the presentation of the older Cyrus, the presentation of Socrates, and the narrated self whose relation to them reveals the problem.

## VII. Founding, monarchy, and Byzantium

The questions of rule and legitimacy are tested in deed. At Byzantium, Xenophon confronts a city, triremes, money, soldiers, unpaid claims, Spartan power, and the possibility of violence against a wholly innocent Greek city.

He does not seize the city or establish unauthorized rule. His restraint carries more evidentiary weight than verbal denial. It bears directly on the preceding questions of knowledge, legitimacy, tyranny, and justice without answering them conclusively.

Justice, prudence, fear of Sparta, concern for Greece, fatherland, kin, self-preservation, and political measure remain mixed. Byzantium is strong deed evidence, not certification of pure motive or complete virtue.

## VIII. Thibron and the final coda

The primary narrative ends institutionally when the army is incorporated under Thibron into renewed Persian war. Its autonomous political existence ends, while private debts, reputations, aims, and Xenophon’s personal homecoming remain unresolved.

Strauss’s final order must remain exact:

1. whether Socrates’ apprehension was vindicated;
2. renewed Persian war for booty;
3. Book VII’s exceptional density of gods and formal oaths.

The ending does not yield a simple return from politics to philosophy, acquisition to piety, or independence to legitimate authority. War, booty, gods, oath, and institutional incorporation remain joined under the unresolved Socrates–Cyrus examination.

Thibron is the primary institutional endpoint. Strauss’s coda is the final textual emphasis. They are not equal rival closures.

## Controlled conclusion

The *Anabasis* remains the center. Through the younger Cyrus and the narrated Xenophon, Xenophon presents political attraction, ascent, command, material governance, founding, restraint, piety, acquisition, and institutional transfer.

Strauss illuminates this presentation by bringing it into relation with Xenophon’s older Cyrus in the *Education of Cyrus* and Xenophon’s Socrates. This relation does not deliver a political model to Xenophon. It reveals Xenophon as the presenter of the problem of justice.

The synthesis does not answer whether knowledge can become legitimate without prescription, whether manly and Socratic virtue can coexist, or whether Xenophon’s self-presentation records achievement, aspiration, approximation, or unresolved claim. It preserves Strauss’s cautious possibility that Xenophon may have regarded himself as the closest approximation best known to himself.
